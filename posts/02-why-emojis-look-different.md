# Why Do Emojis Look Different on iPhone and Android?

You send an emoji that looks friendly on your phone. Your friend shows you the same message, and the face looks a little different. Did the app change what you sent?

**Usually, the emoji text stayed the same. What changed was the artwork used to display it.** Apple, Google, Samsung and other providers can draw their own versions of the same emoji. The receiving app and device decide which design you see.

Think of it like a letter displayed in two fonts: the character can stay the same while its appearance changes. Emoji rendering has a few extra complications, but that distinction explains a lot.

## Same emoji, different artwork

Unicode standardises emoji characters and sequences so software can exchange them. It does not require every platform to use one identical picture. Its [emoji FAQ](https://www.unicode.org/faq/emoji_dingbats.html) explains that vendors control their emoji images and decide which emoji their software supports.

That leaves room for differences in:

- The shape of a face and its eyes.
- The angle of a hand or object.
- Colours, shading and outlines.
- Small details that can make an expression feel different.

So an emoji can be recognisable on both phones without looking exactly alike. A design difference is not, by itself, evidence that your message was corrupted.

## Why “iPhone emoji vs Android emoji” isn’t the whole story

The operating system matters, but the app can matter too. Some software uses the device’s emoji font; other software supplies its own artwork or rendering system. Unicode’s [emoji specification](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr51/) discusses both font-based rendering and other approaches to emoji presentation.

Here are the main pieces:

| What can affect the appearance? | What it means for your message |
|---|---|
| Device and operating system | The installed emoji designs may differ between devices. |
| App or website | It may use its own images instead of the system emoji font. |
| Software version | An update can change artwork or add support for newer emoji. |
| The complete emoji sequence | Extra characters can request a style or combine several parts into one emoji. |

“Android” also covers devices and software from different manufacturers. Don’t assume that every Android phone displays the same artwork—or that every app on one phone must match.

## Does copying an emoji copy its picture?

**When you copy an ordinary text emoji, you copy its character or character sequence—not a screenshot of its design.** The place where you paste it renders that text using the support available there.

For example, copy ❤️ into a note and then into a message. The heart may look different even though you copied the same text. Unicode’s [emoji specification](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr51/) distinguishes the underlying characters and sequences from their visual presentation.

That is also how EmojiSpot’s text-copy buttons work. They copy the emoji text, not an Apple-style or Android-style image. A preview labelled “on your device” shows your rendering environment’s version; it is not a promise about what the recipient will see.

## Why does an emoji sometimes turn black and white?

A black-and-white symbol is not always a broken emoji. Some characters support both **text presentation** and **emoji presentation**.

An invisible variation selector can request a particular style:

- **U+FE0F** requests emoji presentation, typically colourful.
- **U+FE0E** requests text presentation, typically monochrome.

Unicode documents these requests in its [emoji FAQ](https://www.unicode.org/faq/emoji_dingbats.html). They are not a guarantee that every app will draw the symbol identically.

You do not need to type those codes manually. Try copying the complete emoji from a reliable source or selecting it from your emoji keyboard. If it still looks plain in one app, the app’s font or rendering support may be the reason.

## What if it shows a box instead of a different design?

That is a different problem. **A recognisable emoji with different artwork is a presentation difference. An empty box can indicate missing character or font support.** A sequence appearing as several separate symbols can also mean the software does not support its combined form. Unicode describes this fallback behaviour in its [emoji specification](https://www.unicode.org/reports/tr51/).

Updating an app or operating system may help if an available update adds the missing support. It cannot guarantee support for every new emoji: vendors choose their own release schedules.

If your emoji is missing rather than merely different-looking, start by checking whether it displays in another app on the same device. That gives you a useful clue about where the problem occurs.

## Can I make everyone see exactly the same emoji?

Not reliably with plain text. You cannot control every recipient’s software, fonts or display settings.

If the exact artwork matters, an image or screenshot can preserve the design more predictably than a text character. But it is a different kind of content: recipients cannot necessarily treat it like a normal text emoji, and an image may need an accompanying description for accessibility. Check usage rights before republishing someone else’s emoji artwork.

For an everyday message, a few words are usually the simpler solution:

> “That was meant as a friendly joke 😊”

That explains your intention better than hoping everyone sees exactly the same smile.

## The takeaway

**Different-looking emojis do not necessarily mean different text was sent.** The character, the app and the artwork are separate pieces of the experience.

Use words when tone matters, keep your software reasonably up to date, and remember that the design on your screen is only one version of the emoji.
